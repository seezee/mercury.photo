We do a fair amount of concert photography with the Leica M9-P digital rangefinder camera.[^1] This presents certain challenges not affecting the typical DSLR shooter.

[^1]: Since this article posted, we’ve switched to the Fuji XT-1 for performance, action, and low-light digital photography. We also use a Rolleiflex 2.8c, a Hasselblad 503CX, or a Leica MP Classic coupled with a powerful Sunpak 622 Pro strobe for some concert photography.

## Lenses

Chiefly, focal length is limited. Although M-mount lenses are made up to 135mm in length, the longest practical length is 90mm. That’s because above that length, the image inside the viewfinder brightlines is so small that it’s very difficult to focus accurately. Camera shake also becomes an issue at longer focal lengths.

In larger venues where it’s hard to get close to the stage, we prefer a Leica Tele-Elmarit-M 90mm lens. It’s small, fast, and sharp, and cheaper to buy used than the current 90mm lens in Leica’s lineup. On a Leica M3 with its high viewfinder magnification, it’s even better.

If we can get closer, we might use anything from a 21mm to a 50mm lens; it depends on the size of the stage and our distance from it. If we know we’re not going to be reproducing the images at a very large size, we might even use the 50mm at some considerable distance and crop the images in post, since it’s a much faster lens&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;ƒ/1.5 versus ƒ/2.8&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;than the 90mm.

## Light

Then there’s the matter of lighting: to use flash, or not to use flash. Since many very fast lenses are available for the Leica, we don’t use flash&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;since we can’t always get right up to the stage, this works out for the best, anyway. No sense in illuminating the backs of the crowd’s heads instead of your subject, and many venues prohibit flash photography anyway.

Since most stage lights are tungsten, even with colored gels, we either set our white balance to 3200° Kelvin, or, if we can get close enough to the stage, we set it manually with a [WhiBal G7](https://bhpho.to/4q2PxnH){target=_blank rel="external noopener"} pocket grey card.[^2] Even though we often process concert photos in black and white, this saves a step in Adobe Lightroom.

[^2]: Since this article posted, most venues have updated their lights to LEDs. Therefore, it’s better to set the white balance to between 5000º&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&ndash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;5600º <abbr title="Kelvin">K</abbr>.

## Exposure & ISO

We usually set the shutter speed to “aperture priority” and open the lens to its fastest setting. Using the <abbr title="thru-the-lens">TTL</abbr> meter, we aim for a part of the scene that is neither too bright nor too dark and lock exposure by depressing the shutter release to the first stop.[^3] If there’s room, we may use a tripod to steady the shot, compose, and shoot. We’ll “chimp” on the  first shot to check the exposure; if it’s okay, we stop previewing and just shoot (we set previews to “off” in the camera’s settings).

[^3]: Update: Our current practice is to change to exposure priority mode after we’ve determined the correct exposure, so long as the lighting is consistent.
  
Outdoors during daylight hours, set ISO to 160 and use a neutral density filter[^4] if necessary. After dark or indoors, bump that to ISO 800 or 1200. Anything above ISO 1200 may produce unacceptable noise and color shifts. These settings assume a fairly fast lens (ƒ/2.8 and below).

[^4]: A neutral density filter reduces or modifies the intensity of all wavelengths (colors) of light equally, giving no hue change, but reducing luminosity.

Don’t worry about the shots you don’t get: exposure, composition, and focus are all likely “gotchas” that will ruin many of your shots. You’ll probably shoot a lot of pictures, and from those you’ll pick the best ones when it’s over.

## Gaining Access

For a position close to the stage or in other restricted areas, you will need credentials. If you are covering the event for a publication or website, they may issue a you a press I.D. However, many venues require a pass produced and issued by the promoter, venue, or band management. Contact the appropriate person and request credentials: Try the musician’s website (look for a “Press” link), or contact the venue itself. You many find the venue has a general “non-flash photos allowed with a camera phone or point-and-shoot in non-restricted areas” policy, or photography may be prohibited altogether unless they’ve issued you credentials. Find out in advance.

## Etiquette

Remember, other people are trying to see the stage. Others are also photographing. Don’t stand at the front chimping;[^5] move out of the way if you are reviewing your pics. Don’t block the view in any case; stay low, watch where you cast shadows, and be aware of what’s happening around you.

[^5]: Chimping is the habit of checking every photo on a camera display immediately after capture. It by was first written about by <span class="h-card p-name">Robert Deutsch</span>, a <cite>U.S.A. Today</cite> staff photographer, in <time datetime="1999-09">September 1999</time>. He did not invent the term but heard it passed down by word of mouth.

Under no circumstances should you distract the performer. Don’t yell, wave your hands, or do anything to get a performer to look your way; they have enough on their minds without you being a jerk.

## Be Generous

We usually offer the performer a pick of the best images afterward, with the understanding that we retain full rights to the image, and that they need our permission to use them for commercial purposes.

## Have Fun

A stage show is an opportunity to enjoy yourself. You’ll take better pictures if you use your  ears; this is a scientific fact![^6] Relax, enjoy the show, and take pictures.

[^6]: According to Professor Baloney of the Hogwash Institute.
