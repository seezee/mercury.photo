Neo-grunge outfit <span class="h-card p-org">One Gun Solution</span> drove down from Oklahoma City to speak with us <time datetime="2013-01-13">earlier this month</time>. We met them <time datetime="2012-10-20">last October</time> at the CD release party for their freshman release <cite>Perceptions</cite>, after [Kill the Reflection](/blog/kill-reflection-interview-gallery/) singer <span class="h-card p-name">Matt Routt</span> invited us.

Short and muscular, frontman and vocalist <span class="h-card p-name">Dave Washburn</span>’s keen stare could back down the most belligerent drunk in the bars he frequently plays. His shaved head is contrasted by a mighty beard, which also appears in the band logo. He may look like a biker, but he reveals a bookish, intellectual bent during the interview.

Tall, lean, and laconic, guitarist <span class="h-card p-name">Matt Francis</span> is soft-spoken and thoughtful. He conducts himself with polite southern manners suggesting a kind of upbringing one rarely encounters these days.

Gruff-voiced, fast-talking bassist <span class="h-card p-name">Raymond Dick</span> is the group’s trickster, usually the first to make an amusing crack at his fellow’s expense, unless drummer <span class="h-card p-name">Jim Paterson</span> beats him to it. He’s a new father; if you see him at a show, be sure to congratulate him.

Paterson looks like he wants to kill his drums when he’s playing, but in the flesh, he’s personable and funny. Like all the band members, he’s passionate about music.

<span class="spkr">Mercury Photo Bureau</span> How’d you guys meet?

<span class="spkr">Jim Paterson</span> I was in a country band with this really horrible singer, and Ray walked in to try out for the band. He started playing <cite class="short-work">[Master of Puppets](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/album/master-of-puppets-remastered/1275819392?i=1275819878&mt=1&app=music&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external nofollow noopener"}</cite> […] and I started playing along with him, and he was like, <q>Oh, really?</q> and that’s when we decided to go away from country […].

<span class="spkr">Raymond Dick</span> After a few lineup changes, we ended up with Dave and Matt&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> Matt came first&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;he was with our last band, with our other singer.

<span class="spkr">Matt Francis</span> I was actually at Guitar Center, with my wife and little girl&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> His little girl actually introduced us.

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> Yes, she was just staring at Jim, and I was just there getting guitar strings, just getting back into guitar playing.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> She was lookin’ around at her mama&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> Jimmy was doin’ the p&aelig;dophile deal, lookin’ at the little baby! [*laughter*]

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-01-31-one-gun-solution/czahller-20121020-23-41-35.jpg", "img-constrained", "Ray plays bass; Dave sings. Dave holds the microphone, on the stand, in both hands.", "Raymond & Dave (III)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> That might wind up in the interview [transcript], so be careful what you say.

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> Scratch that, I’m sorry! [*More laughter*]

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> I may just describe you guys as jocular, and not above having a joke at one another’s expense.

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> You ought to see our rehearsals.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> How did Dave wind up in the band?

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> After we got Matt, we were using <span class="h-card p-org">Left to Die</span>’s singer, <span class="h-card p-name">Jeremy Waller</span>. We jammed for a while, doing the heavier things, but after a while, we decided to go more musical&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> And we needed a singer that was dedicated to our band, not split between two bands […].

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> And he needed to have a wider range of musical types that he could relate to, and articulate.

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> That’s how we became One Gun Solution. Dave came with the name; when Dave came, we changed everything. We changed genres one-hundred percent.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> What were the challenges when you formed the band?

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> [When we first got together,] me and Dave didn’t see eye-to-eye. And that’s because we didn’t know each other.

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> That’s ’cause you’re 6&prime; 3&Prime; and he’s, like, 5&prime; 2&Prime;. [*laughter*]

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> Then we […] got to […] <q>This is more important than just, the way we feel about each other,</q> we just needed to learn more about each other to figure out who we were or where we were coming from […].

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-01-31-one-gun-solution/czahller-20121020-23-40-11.jpg", "img-constrained", "Dave smiles and gestures.", "Dave (V)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> How would you describe your musical genre before Dave?

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> Speed metal, thrash.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> Hardcore metal.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> If I were an <abbr title="Artists and Repertoire">A&R</abbr> man and you were trying to get me to sign you, how would you describe your music?

<span class="spkr">Dave Washburn</span> Music for everyone. We write tunes we love, and, hopefully, everyone else will jump on board.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> If I were that A&R guy and you came to me with that thirty-second elevator pitch, I would not return your call, because I do not know what that means.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> We actually cover a broad spectrum of musical styles, even soft rock&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;borderline pop-rock&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;all the way up to hard rock. Not metal; it’s just short of being metal. We do have some metal elements, like some of our guitar riffs might be a little metal, but because of the tones we use, they have a softer sound&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> Nothing that you might [not] hear on an <span class="h-card p-org">Alice in Chains</span> song.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> How did you become musicians?

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> […] I grew up in L.A., just seven miles south of Hollywood, and everybody out there’s in a band; I mean everybody. I traded my bicycle when I was thirteen for my first bass […].

When I came here, everybody was a little more laid back; I mean, it was easier to do, ’cause there wasn’t a million people doing it, you know? It’s always been something I’ve been interested in.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> He couldn’t handle being a male prostitute. [*laughter*] I started in the 80s; I was just starting to get into music. My brother played drums. I started watching bands like <span class="h-card p-org">Def Leppard</span>, and it really intrigued me&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;<span class="h-card p-name">Rick Allen</span>[^1] only had one arm&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;I went from there, I started listening to <span class="h-card p-org">Rush</span>.

[^1]: Def Leppard drummer.

I’d heard them before, but I’d never listened to them as a drummer. And that’s when I first actually picked up the drums, because, before then, I was air drumming, because I couldn’t afford a full drum set. [Then] I started playing Rush tunes and I delved into progressive metal and progressive rock.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-01-31-one-gun-solution/czahller-20121020-23-01-22.jpg", "img-constrained", "Jim beats the skins", "Jim (III)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Rush were known for oddball time signatures. Is that what appealed to you?

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> It was all of it; it was the fills that weren’t normal, generic fills; it stepped outside of the normal borders of a 4/4 time signature and it appealed to my [intellect].

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> I’m from Texas, and for me it just started when I first heard a song on the radio. First three concerts I ever went to were <span class="h-card p-org">ZZ Top</span> (my dad took me). I remember as a kid, I had four sisters, growing up, just all in the back of our van and singing along [with the radio].

I’ve done so many things in my life, but I’ve always come back to music […]; there’s nothing else that really completes everything for me.

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> He couldn’t even handle the porn. [*laughter*]

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> I got my first guitar whenever I was about twelve years old, and my mom bought it for me; it was a [Sears special](https://www.chicagomag.com/Chicago-Magazine/The-312/May-2012/How-Sears-Changed-the-Sound-of-American-Music-Twice/){target=_blank rel="external nofollow noopener"} with the little speaker in it, good for in the bars; an electrician rewired it for us and I got some new pickups in it. I never had any formal lessons, until I was about thirteen [when] I had two [lessons], and by then I’d gotten a better guitar. I started playing because it was an outlet for my emotions and I could articulate what I was feeling […].

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> I’ve been a writer since I was in about […] third grade; [that’s when] I wrote my first short story, and it got published and [I read] it aloud to the whole school […].

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> I take it that you write most of the song lyrics?

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> Yes.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> He won’t let anybody else write ’em.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Matt, could you tell me what was going on that you wanted to express through music?

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> Well, just [the craziness] at school; we moved around a lot and Dad left [when] I was young. It was just a crazy time […], so [music] was just an outlet for me […]; other people use other things. Actually, the main acoustic riff in <cite class="short-work">Here on After,</cite> that’s the first thing I ever wrote; I wrote that when I was thirteen.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-01-31-one-gun-solution/czahller-20121020-23-05-03.jpg", "img-constrained", "Dave leans back, microphone in both hands, grimacing as he sings.", "Dave (III)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Dave, some of the lyrics are pretty straightforward; some of the lyrics are opaque. <cite class="short-work">Underneath the Skin</cite> is right out there; it’s about p&aelig;dophilia in the church, but some of the others might benefit from some exposition: I’m thinking of <cite class="short-work">The Fray</cite> and <cite class="short-work">Down the Rabbit Hole,</cite> which seems to be about a girl contemplating suicide.

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> Absolutely. I’m big on stories; that’s what I love about country music. […] A lot of times [the band] will come up with a riff or a whole song, and I’ll come in and figure out how I’m going to approach it. I listen to the song a million times and try to find [a subject].

[<cite class="short-work">The Fray</cite> is] my favorite track [on <cite>Perceptions</cite>], aside from <cite class="short-work">Here on After.</cite> [It’s] about being in an abusive domestic situation. You always feel stuck [in this type of arrangement]; your friends tell you to [to get out of it]; you hear, but you don’t listen; you stay for whatever reason.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Is <cite class="short-work">Here on After</cite> the sequel to <cite class="short-work">The Fray</cite>?

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> No; do you remember that old movie <cite>Carrie</cite>? That whole song is about that.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> When you listen to the instrumental part the band brings you, do you look for a particular emotion to serve as a jumping-off point for a narrative?

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> Well, thankfully, [I’m] in a band where there’s so much chemistry […], particularly [between] Matt and I […]; we’re like peas and carrots. I don’t know what it is, but whenever we’re together, everything just seems to fall in place, and it’s so easy to write together.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> While we were having drinks before the interview, it came up that you want to re-record a couple of the songs from <cite>Perceptions</cite> and re-issue it.

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> The album was just really rushed. We were recording with <span class="h-card p-name">Provo Provenzano</span> at [Pirated Sound Studios](https://www.bandmine.com/artist/music/piratedsounds/859336){target=_blank rel="external nofollow noopener"} in Purcell; he’s one of the best ears in the state. We had a deadline: we had the CD release show, and we bit off more than we could chew […].

After listening [to <cite>Perceptions</cite> repeatedly] over the last few months, we want to go back [and fix some of the tracks].

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> We recorded <cite class="short-work">Rise Against</cite> without Provo, so we didn’t get the full remastering to CD, and there are some problems on it.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Who do you admire or emulate, musically?

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> <span class="h-card p-name">Cliff Burton</span>,[^2] <span class="h-card p-name">Steve Harris</span>.[^3] Probably them two, as far as, like, original influences, from when I first started playing bass. They’ll always be my heroes, but as far as nowadays, talking about the *sound* of the bass, I like <span class="h-card p-org">Tool</span>[^4] and <span class="h-card p-org">Chevelle</span>.[^5] As far as [technique], I’m still influenced by Steve Harris. I like the new sound with the old influences.

[^2]: The late bassist for <span class="h-card p-org">Metallica</span>.

[^3]: <span class="h-card p-org">Iron Maiden</span> bassist.

[^4]: Tool’s bassist is <span class="h-card p-name">Paul d’Amour</span>.

[^5]: Chevelle’s bassist is <span class="h-card p-name">Geno Lenardo</span>.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> These guys will tell you I pretty much live <span class="h-card p-org">Dream Theater</span>. <span class="h-card p-name">Mike Portnoy</span> *is* my favorite drummer. [I also look to Rush drummer] <span class="h-card p-name">Neil Peart</span>.

But, it doesn’t matter who it is; whatever music I’m listening to, I try to feel what’s inside it. Like, whenever Ray plays bass, I don’t sit and go, <q>Well, okay, I’ve gotta play a 4/4 time signature.</q> I play what I feel. […]

<span class="h-card p-name">Danny Carey</span>[^6] is [a gifted poly-rhythmic drummer]. Mike Portnoy is […] phenomenal […] all-around. I look at [what useful things] they bring […] and add them to [my personal technique].

[^6]: Tool’s drummer.

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> Hands down, I’m a huge grunge-head […]. The 90s were just awesome for me [; I was a teenager then]. <span class="h-card p-name">Eddie Vedder</span>; back in his prime, he was just untouchable. His story-telling, his presence; he could do anything and still carry a note.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-01-31-one-gun-solution/czahller-20121020-23-00-27.jpg", "img-constrained", "Matt smiles as he plays guitar.", "Matt (I)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> [Guitarist] <span class="h-card p-name">Nuno Bettencourt</span> from <span class="h-card p-org">Extreme</span>; now he writes and plays with <span class="h-card p-name p-given-name">Rihanna</span>; he’s definitely a huge influence. <span class="h-card p-name">Jimi Hendrix</span>, <span class="h-card p-name">Steve Vai</span>, <span class="h-card p-name">Joe Satriani</span>, <span class="h-card p-name">Paul Gilbert</span>.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> With the exception of Hendrix, I’d classify these guitarists as technicians, you know, somewhat clinical in their approach to playing. Do you put emotional expressiveness ahead of technique?

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> Always. That always comes before the technical stuff. [Those guitarists], and I want to mention <span class="h-card p-name">Eddie Van Halen</span> as well, they were very technical, but you could always hear the stories they were telling. Whatever the song title was, you could hear it, you could totally picture it, whether it was an instrumental or whether [it was a vocal number]. You can play scales all over the place, but the true greats articulate their emotions through their playing.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> One person who lacked that was <span class="h-card p-name">Yngwie Malmsteen</span>. He was fast; he was bad; but after about sixty seconds, I feel like I’ve [heard it]. It’s like, [*speaks in loud falsetto, provoking laughter*] <q>Look what I can do! Look what I can do!</q>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> How do you take something as specific as grunge and incorporate it into your singing without sounding dated?

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> I don’t really worry [about that]. I do what appeals to me and makes me happy. Every song, every note, you’re in the moment. It’s never scripted for us. At the end of every rehearsal, we always leave an hour […] to jam, just to see what happens; that’s where some of our best songs have come from. We don’t set out to sound like anybody; it just comes out the way it does […].

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> I’m a gear-head, as you can probably tell by the cameras and A/V equipment [*gestures around room*]. Tell me about your favorite piece of musical equipment.

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> <span class="h-card p-org">Mesa</span> Boogie [amplifiers].

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> I agree with that […]; I love that low end of those Mesas, especially when you stack them […].

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> For me, it would be my homemade drums […]. I took a <span class="h-card p-org">DW Pacific</span> kit [and] took all the hardware off of it, made my own shells and painted them and everything. They’re <span class="h-card p-org">Keller</span> shells, which is what DW used before <time datetime="1999">’99</time>. […] So it’s basically a high-end drum kit that I made myself.[^7]

[^7]: A lengthy discussion of Jim’s deep bass drum follows, eventually degrading into various off-color double-entendres before we change the subject.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Could you walk me through the lyrics of <cite class="short-work">Needle and Thread</cite>?

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> <cite class="short-work">Needle and Thread</cite> is about exposing yourself […]. You can be anybody on the outside, but you know who you are on the inside, and exposing that [makes you vulnerable].

[*We took a lavatory break at this point, but left the recorder running&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;while we wer gone, there was discussion of Dave’s beard, during which he revealed that he was contemplating shaving it off. When asked <q>Why?</q> he gave this answer:*]

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> […] Yeah, I got a really good jawline, too&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;[*laughter*]

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> That’s all it is! <q>I need to look better! I got a purty mouth!</q>

[*Interview resumes.*]

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> None of you are originally from Oklahoma. How did you wind up here?

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> I played in two bands [in California, then I] gave it up for about eight or nine years. [There were] family problems; all my mom’s family was out here already. [My mom] was just […] a wild child; she moved to California [and] had me and my brother out there, and then, [when I was] fourteen or fifteen, she got sick. [That’s when] we all moved out here […].

I was the only one in my family born in California; everyone else is from Oklahoma.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> I was born and raised in western New York just south of Buffalo, on Lake Erie. [I grew up poor. I graduated from high school at age seventeen and] went straight into the military. Was in the U.S. army for eight years; I got out at Fort Sill and I spent the next three years [in] Oklahoma.

I decided to go back New York to be with family; after ten years of that, I said, <q>I have to be away from family,</q> and that’s [how I came back here.]

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> I was born and raised in Arlington, Texas; I’ve been all over the place. I ended up […] in Oklahoma in <time datetime="2007">2007</time>; my father had moved up here previously from Texas, for work. I was actually passing through, but I decided […] to stop in, spend some time with my old man […], and I ended up getting a job.

I went to go fill a prescription one day, in Edmond, and my [future] wife was behind the counter, “dealin’ drugs,” and she had a lot of nice [tattoos]. So I noticed that, and [it gave me an excuse] to say, <q>Hello.</q> I actually left, and I went out to my truck, and she [later] told me that she was speaking to [a coworker], and she was like, <q>Man, that guy was really nice, but he didn’t even ask me for my number. I’ll never see him again; what a dirt bag!</q> [So I called the pharmacy from my truck,] and I was like, <q>Hey, this is Dave; I just picked up the script in there; remember me?</q>; she was like, <q>Yeah!</q> I was like, <q>Can I take you out some time?</q> […]

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> I was born in Nüremberg, Germany; I lived there for two years. We got back to the states after my dad got out and moved up and down the whole east coast […]. Moved to south Texas for a while and ended up in Arizona, Illinois, Missouri, ended up here in Oklahoma because some of my mom’s family had moved [here] from West Virginia […].

I’ve left several times; I was in the army myself. I ended up back here after I got out of the army, and I’ve been here since.

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> All three of us [*gestures to himself, Matt, and Jim*] are ex-military.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> What other bands have each of you played in? What’s your earliest involvement with music?

<span class="spkr">Dick</span> My very first band was called <span class="h-card p-org">Mentally Disturbed</span> [; that was around] <time datetime="1991">’91</time>. Then I moved out here, and I was in a band called <span class="h-card p-org">Omission</span>, [then] <span class="h-card p-org">Carnage</span>, [plus a couple with the guys from] <span class="h-card p-org"><abbr title="Left to Die">LTD</abbr></span>.

[*The band digresses into a discussion of trying to match a backing vocalist to Dave’s singing. Jim suggests that Raymond should sing backup, saying that* <q>he sounds like he’s gargling razor blades.</q>]

More recently, before [One Gun Solution], I was in […] <span class="h-card p-org">Curbside Attraction</span>.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> I was in chorus from second grade to eleventh. I was kicked out because my voice dropped too low for bass II. Puberty hit me really hard.

[My first band was when I was in the military], at Fort Campbell, Kentucky […]. We were all into Metallica. We started just playing covers; I think we wrote one song; it was called <cite class="short-work">Kill the Dogs</cite> […].

While I was at Fort Campbell, I was just playing with random people at the rec center, you know, just getting a wide variety of styles from everybody who was walking into the “jam” room. When I got to Germany, I really didn’t do anything with anybody, other than I had a friend who played guitar, and he was into Metallica; he was into <span class="h-card p-org">Extreme</span> […].

In the summer of <time datetime="1996">1996</time>, I got into a country band in Germany; most of the people in the band were German. Then I got told I was leaving Germany, so I had to cut it short.

When I came back to the states, I didn’t [play any music] until about <time datetime="2005">2005</time>. I was working for <span class="h-card p-org">Penny Saver</span> or <span class="h-card p-org">Thrifty Nickel</span> in New York; I actually got the job because I’d answered an ad for a drum tryout; well, they tried out a bunch of different drummers, and I was [the one they called], because, they said, I fit better, you know, […] I have a better “feeling” than some drummers. I played with them for about 4 or 5 years, and it just fizzled out.

And after that, there’s a country singer out there named Todd Andrews; he’s signed with Tate Music Group[^8]; I was with him for a few months before I moved to Oklahoma. And then I joined a band called <span class="h-card p-org">Sin for Sin</span>; that’s what [the bandleader] called it, but we couldn’t use [the name], because somebody else was already using it. [After that, I met Ray.]

[^8]: Tate Music Group is located in Mustang, Oklahoma.

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> I guess my first real music project was in elementary school. I […] played the stand-up bass. I […] grew up just a block away from the Pantera house[^9] in Dalworthington Gardens. There’s two small cities in Arlington[, Texas], Pantego and Dalworthington Gardens, and I used to carry that sucker; I had to walk home from school, and [the bass] was three times the size of me.

[^9]: A house once shared by the members of the rock band <span class="h-card p-org">Pantera</span>.

I was very into opera, classical music; […] to this day, I still love to [hear classical music played live].

I always wanted to be [a singer in a band]&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;not that I had an addiction to being the center of attention or anything, but I just love to please; I love to entertain. So I was just in various, kind of unmentionable musical projects; actually, one of my favorite bands from Arlington [is] <span class="h-card p-org">Spoonfed Tribe</span>; there’s like, thirteen, fourteen, sixteen of them, depending on what show you see them; I always wanted to be a part of that; never could, but they’re real good friends […].

[I] came here; was in a little project called <span class="h-card p-org">Black Water</span>; and then, there was a previous One Gun Solution project; that’s how I came with the name. It was more of a progressive metal band. Metal meets Tool type of thing […]. Then I met these guys […].

<span class="spkr">Francis</span> [I played viola in middle school orchestra]. I did that for several years, until I picked up guitar, and my brother and another fella that we grew up with […] started a band, <span class="h-card p-org">Aunt Mary’s Ant Farm</span>; it had kind of a “jam” band kind of a feel. And we played on and off, together, until, *shoot*, <time datetime="2005">2005</time> or <time datetime="2006">’06</time>. Our bass player, Josh, graduated from college and moved to Japan […]. My brother graduated [and got his engineering licenses]. So we all went our separate ways.

I didn’t play guitar for several years, and finally ended up meeting these guys; got into Curbside Attraction with Jim and Ray, and Dave came shortly after.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Have you toured yet?

<span class="spkr">Washburn</span> No, man; it’s all local.

<span class="spkr">Paterson</span> We have played in Dallas, but we all have jobs, so it’s tough to schedule a tour. We’d like to, when it becomes feasible.
