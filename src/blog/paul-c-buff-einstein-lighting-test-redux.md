In continuing to learn the ins and outs of our recently acquired Paul C. Buff “Einstein” (model E640) studio strobe, we’re teaching ourselves to meter flash. The Sekonic L-308S Flashmate exposure meter has three measuring modes:

* Ambient
* Auto reset cordless flash
* Corded flash

For this lighting test, we used the **auto eset** mode, since the subject was too far from the strobe to connect a sync cable. To meter the light, we attached a Yongnuo receiver to the strobe’s trigger port via a short sync cable, then fired it with an inexpensive Yongnuo transmitter. We later installed the transmitter in the camera hot shoe to light the portrait. To calculate the exposure, we followed this procedure:

1. Set the meter to match the camera ISO &amp; shutter speed (160 and <sup>1</sup>&frasl;<sub>180</sub> second, respectively)
2. Set the measuring mode to auto reset&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;for the next 90 seconds, the meter will measure only when flash is activated
3. Ensured that the plastic “Lumidisc” was positioned over the meter’s lens
4. Moved to where we would be standing for the self portrait
5. Pointed the meter at the camera &amp; depressed the "measure" button
6. With our free hand, used the transmitter to trigger the strobe
7. Adjusted the camera aperture to match the meter reading

## The Portrait

The tableau was inspired by a friend’s remark, made when we shared the <a href="" rel="prev">[previous lighting post](/blog/paul-c-buff-einstein-lighting-test/)</a> on social media. He thought the author looked like [<span class="h-card p-name">James Joyce</span>](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ef/James_Joyce_by_Alex_Ehrenzweig,_1915_restored.jpg){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}, so we took the idea and ran with it. We dug up some antique eyeglasses similar to a pair we knew Joyce once wore and added a pork pie hat (the same Dobbs model worn by [this famous singer](https://elvisdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Elvis-1960-Lincoln-2.jpg){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}). The jacket, which has a vintage brass compass pinned to it, suggested hunting, as did the silk-screened duck design in the hat lining, so we added the duck decoys, the antique bourbon bottle, our Billingham camera bag (which resembles a hunter’s bag), and also the shotgun. Let us save you the trouble of pointing out that a 12-gauge shotgun is not a bird gun; we know the difference.

We made basic tonal adjustments, then converted the image to black and white and split-toned it. Finally, we added some vignetting to complete the “old time” feel of the image.
