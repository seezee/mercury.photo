The Red Dirt Rangers may not have birthed Red Dirt, but they helped midwife the musical genre into the world. And like so much Oklahoma music, Red Dirt has made its influence felt far beyond the state’s borders.{.drop-cap}

Starting in the 1930s, when Kansas City Jazz arose from the ashes of the Oklahoma City Blue Devils,[^1] Oklahoma has been ground zero for several important American popular musical styles. There’s jazz-guitarist <span class="h-card p-name">Charlie Christian</span>, born in Texas but raised in Oklahoma City, whose horn-like playing paved the way for the modern guitar sound&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;influencing not just jazz, blues, western swing, and rock guitarists, but a whole passel of non-guitarist musicians as well.[^2]

[^1]:Originally located in the city’s Deep Deuce district, the Blue Devils operated from <time datetime="1923">1923</time> until <time datetime="1935">1935</time>, when their former pianist, <span class="h-card p-name">William <span class="p-nickname">“Count”</span> Basie</span> poached most of their best players, including <span class="h-card p-name">Lester Young</span>, <span class="h-card p-name">Walter Page</span>, and <span class="h-card p-name">Jimmy Rushing</span>, for his newly formed orchestra. The Blue Devils had relocated to Kansas City <time datetime="1930">five years earlier</time>.

[^2]: Black Sabbath’s first song, <cite class="short-work">A Song for Jim</cite> was <q>an absolute Charlie Christian takeoff.</q>&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;<cite class="h-card p-name cite-person">Jim Simpson</span>, quoted in <cite class="h-card p-name cite-person">Martin Popoff</cite>’s <cite>Black Sabbath FAQ</cite> (Backbeat Books, <time datetime="2011">2011</time>).

Western swing got its start at Tulsa’s Cain’s Ballroom, where couples danced to <span class="h-card p-org"><span class="h-card p-name">Bob Wills</span> and His Texas Playboys</span>. A few decades later <span class="h-card p-name">Leon Russell</span> converted a church into a studio; soon after, Russell and his Tulsa Mob gave rock ’n’ roll radio stations around the world the “Tulsa Sound”&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;influencing musicians as far away as British rockers <span class="h-card p-name">Eric Clapton</span> and <span class="h-card p-name">Mark Knopfler</span>. Clapton played various Tulsa venues so often in the <time datetime="1978">1970s</time>[^3] that critic <span class="h-card p-name">Robert Christgau</span> wrote <q>Whatever Eric isn’t anymore&nbsp;&hellip; he’s certainly king of the Tulsa sound.</q>[^4]

[^3]: Clapton’s band at the time consisted of Tulsans <span class="h-card p-name">Carl Radle</span>, <span class="h-card p-name">Dick Sims</span>, and <span class="h-card p-name">Jamie Oldaker</span>.

[^4]: <cite class="h-card p-name cite-person">Christgau, Robert</cite>, <cite>Rock Albums of the ’70s: A Critical Guide</cite> (Da Capo Press, <time datetime="1990">1990</time>)

In <cite>From Blue Devils to Red Dirt: The Colors of Oklahoma Music</cite>, author <span class="h-card p-name">John Wooley</span> speculates that Oklahoma’s geographically central location, with <q>music from the rest of the  USA swirling through it from all sides,</q> was one factor in the creative ferment going on in the state throughout the twentieth century. Whatever it was&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;something in the air and water, outlaw genes passed down from the Sooners, the state’s geographic and demographic diversity&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;the music has always traveled in both directions. Think of how dust blowing in from the northern plains caused a young <span class="h-card p-name"><span class="p-nickname">Woody</span> Guthrie</span> to hit the road with his guitar and pen. Every schoolchild in the nation knows the first verse to <cite class="short-work">This Land Is Your Land.</cite>

Another such musical style, Red Dirt, began At <span class="h-card p-name">Bob Childers</span>’ home (“The Farm”) in Stillwater in the <time datetime="1972">1970s</time>. Along with Childers, <span class="h-card p-name">Jimmy LaFave</span>, and <span class="h-card p-name">Tom Skinner</span>, the Red Dirt Rangers got their start at The Farm. The rambling five-room home served as a musical commune for Red Dirt musicians for two decades. <q>Everybody lived there.</q>[^5] The Farm burned down in 2003, but the music lives on.

[^5]: Author conversation with John Cooper via Facebook Messenger, <time datetime="2019-10-21T18:20:00-5:00">21 October 2018</time>.

<blockquote>

The Farm was as much an attitude as a physical structure. It allowed a setting where freedom rang and all things were possible. Out of this setting came the music.
  <footer>&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;<cite>John Cooper</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
  
Comprising guitarists and singers <span class="h-card p-name">Brad Piccolo</span> and <span class="h-card p-name">Ben Han</span> and mandolinist and singer <span class="h-card p-name">John Cooper</span>,[^6] the Rangers have played every Woodyfest except in <time datetime="2004">2004</time>, when they barely survived a helicopter crash near Cushing that killed the pilot and a passenger. Piccolo did appear and play a few songs that year,[^7] so you could say their streak is unbroken. Their Thursday night main stage performance marks their twentieth (or twenty-first, depending on how you’re counting) Woodyfest appearance, apart from the times they’ve supported other guest artists.

[^6]: Drummer <span class="h-card p-name">Rick Gomez</span>, bassist <span class="h-card p-name">Don Morris</span>, and fiddler and multi-instrumentalist <span class="h-card p-name">Randy Crouch</span> round out the band.

[^7]: Piccolo was still healing from several broken ribs, knee damage, lacerations, and a broken coccyx.
