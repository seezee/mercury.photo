<span class="h-card p-org">[Kali Ra](http://officialkalira.com){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}</span> front man <span class="h-card p-name">David Goad</span> recently sat down with us to answer some questions.[^1] His lovely wife <span class="h-card p-name p-given-name">Alicia</span> was on hand to <del>call his bullshit</del> <ins>supplement his answers and fact-check</ins>. She also told us <cite class="short-work">10 Things You Didn’t Know about David Goad,</cite> which you can read in [Part 2](/blog/kali-ra-interview-pt-2/#ten-things-you-didn-t-know-about-david-goad){rel=next}.

[^1]: Goad also leads various other musical and performance projects.

<span class="spkr">Mercury Photo Bureau</span> I understand you’re a graduate of <abbr title="Academy of Contemporary Music at the University of Central Oklahoma">ACM</abbr>. When did you graduate?

<span class="spkr">David Goad</span> [*Looks at watch*] Let’s see … I graduated in <time datetime="2011">2011</time>.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Were you a composition major?

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> I was a guitar performance major. [I took composition classes at] ACM and <abbr title="Oklahoma City Community College">OCCC</abbr> […]

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Were you involved in musical theatre?

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> No, [my area of study] was musical pedagogy; I mostly studied classical guitar, classical voice, and composition theory.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2012-11-27-kali-ra-pt-1/czahller-20121013-23-03-52.jpg", "img-constrained", "David walks onstage at the HiLo Club, expression stern. He is photographed from a low angle. The image is black-and-white.", "Enter David" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> You’ve had a couple of your compositions used in theatre and television.

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> I had a piece used on [nationally syndicated PBS show] <cite>Two Wheel Oklahoma</cite>, and I’ve also written a [30-minute] score for a [90-minute] theatre piece called <cite>Fubar</cite>[, produced at <abbr title="University of Central Oklahoma">UCO</abbr> in <time datetime="2011">2011</time>].

For the PBS show, the producers wanted music that was [both] driving and ambient; I can do both of those things. The piece is called called <cite class="short-work">Aphasia</cite> and it’s a meditation on a pentatonic scale[^2]&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;for the first half of the piece&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;and then you have a lot of “roboto” rhythm, and there are bridges with a lot of sharp punctuations. The instrumentation is a lot of tympani and a lot of percussion-like vibraphone and marimba. But I wrote the melody for guitar with a “sustainer” pickup,[^3] allowing the performer to play without picking notes [with the right hand]; you hold the strings to the frets [with your left hand] […] [so you can play the guitar like a violin].

[^2]: A musical scale with five notes per octave. Scales in modern Western music typically employ a heptatonic (seven-note) scale.

[^3]: Sustainers comprise a device to generate a magnetic field (causing the strings to vibrate) coupled with a pickup to “pick up” and amplify the sound, thus creating near-infinite sustain.

It’s a densely harmonic piece; in the second part, it’s a <sup>12</sup>⁄<sub>8</sub> rhythm; it’s bouncier with a funky bass line that’s static through the whole thing. Then it becomes natural minor and carries it all the way to the end, where it ends on the tonic, [on a major chord].

[The music for] <cite>Fubar</cite> was a lot less consonant. It was mostly incidental music. I used a lot of twelve-tone and atonal techniques, with a lot of minor seconds. I was trying to develop a sonic ambiance based on sympathetic vibration, rather than a cohesive harmony or melody, vibrations that would stretch or pulsate rapidly.

[The theatrical piece] <cite>Fubar</cite> was experimental, like <span class="h-card p-name">Antonin Artaud</span>’s **Theatre of Cruelty**.[^4] So, obviously, I wanted to compose something “deconstructionist,” taking a very non-musical approach to music. [Conversely,] I would “give the audience a bone” here and there. I created some very “robotic” dance pieces driven by distorted bass and drums. It’s sort of […] <span class="h-card p-name">Brian Eno</span> meets <span class="h-card p-name">Trent Reznor</span> meets <span class="h-card p-name">John Cage</span> […].

[^4]: Artaud theorized the surrealist theatrical form <span class="foreign" lang="fr">Théâtre de la Cruauté</span> in his essay collection <cite>The Theatre and Its Double</cite>, published 1938.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Tell us about your pre&ndash;Kali Ra projects, and also your current side projects.

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> Before Kali Ra, I was in <span class="h-card p-org">Of the Tower</span>. I don’t want to say we were […] a cliché Goth band, but we were more Goth than Kali Ra.

Of the Tower consisted of <span class="h-card p-name">Todd Plunkett</span>, <span class="h-card p-name">Nathan Steinman</span>, and me. In the 90s and the early aughts, [Todd and Nathan] were part of the Oklahoma rock scene. Nathan was in <span class="h-card p-org">Venditos</span> and Todd was in  <span class="h-card p-org">Kite Flying Robot</span> and  <span class="h-card p-org">Ghosts of Monkshood</span>, and he was also in  <span class="h-card p-org">Shiny Toy Guns</span> for a bit. He was everywhere.

We were trying to approach the band as an art collective, and we had very high aspirations for it, but it just never happened […]. Musically, we all had similar influences, and we wanted to cram them together to make delicious jam out of it. We were all fans of  <span class="h-card p-org">Bauhaus</span> and <span class="h-card p-org">The Doors</span> and <span class="h-card p-name">David Bowie</span> and  <span class="h-card p-org">Joy Division</span> and <span class="h-card p-name">Philip Glass</span>. We wanted to do this minimalist post-punk thing that was dark and Goth-y.

I took my guitar approach and my singing from <span class="h-card p-name">Daniel Ash</span> and <span class="h-card p-name">Peter Murphy</span>, and Todd did a lot of Joy Division&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&ndash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;<span class="h-card p-org">New Order</span> stuff, and like, <span class="h-card p-name">[John] Densmore</span> from The Doors; very expressive, angular drumming; it was almost jazzy. Nathan was heavily into world music: klezmer and Gypsy music and Philip Glass. You’d think a mixture of all three of them [would work]. It was much more difficult in reality to effectively meld them together […].

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2012-11-27-kali-ra-pt-1/czahller-20121013-23-05-33.jpg", "img-constrained", "David holds the microphone, free of the microphone stand, in his right hand. His left grips the stand. He is singing. The image is black-and-white.", "David (I)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">Alicia Goad</span> I really don’t think that [how they collaborated] was what broke [Of the Tower] up. I think it was more [a matter] of them not coming to practice and not wanting to move on and tour. It wasn’t because of creative differences.

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> I will admit I was being diplomatic. It was my aspiration to tour and produce albums […] and now that Of the Tower was disbanded, I had <span class="foreign" lang="la">tabula rasa</span> to be able to hone in on the songwriting craft, to not be sticking to a pastiche and [to] write the best songs that I could. [Being] in a band for so many years, that had almost been credible, you know, it was a good learning [experience]. So now I had this ability to write baroque, pop-rock-electro-glam-industrial infused songs. Kali Ra is the next step, creatively, and is also my flagship project.

I now have [many excellent musicians at my disposal]. [<span class="h-card p-name">Justin Hogan</span>](/blog/psychic-milk-interview-gallery/) won the <time datetime="2002">2002</time> Best Guitarist Oklahoma Rock Award […]. <span class="h-card p-name">Joel Price</span>, another friend and colleague, is an excellent producer; his production style is like Brian Eno’s. [He thinks in terms of] soundscapes, to create music as a painting […].

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Let me sidetrack you with a question I recently asked [<span class="h-card p-name p-nickname">Peelander Yellow</span>](/blog/peelander-z-halloween/). Brian Eno once wrote an [essay](https://web.archive.org/web/19991116205309/https://www.wired.com/wired/archive/7.01/eno.html){target=_blank rel="external noopener"} in which he posited that recent advances in recording technology rob music-making of intimacy and immediacy, replacing what were once one-to-one correspondences between action and result (“analogue”) with multi-step abstractions that require the assistance of a software engineer for even the simplest task (“digital”). What technology do you employ when recording, and has this been a problem for you?

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> Right now, we’re using the washing machine, the CD rack, and, um, rooms; we’re very heavy into using rooms, along with all the digital stuff. [*laughter*]

My point is, we try to meld the two together and try not to rely too heavily [on either analog or digital]. That’s where Joel excels; he and I do a lot of experimentation with timbre, pitch, and sonic depth, things that were commonplace in industrial and electronic music at its inception, but are now passé [as musicians increasingly rely on <abbr title="Virtual Studio Technology">VST</abbr> plugins].[^5] By the way, Joel is also Kali Ra’s other guitarist; his style is similar to <span class="h-card p-name">Robert Fripp</span>’s or <span class="h-card p-name p-nickname">The Edge</span>’s.

[^5]: Virtual Studio Technology is an interface for integrating software audio synthesizer and effect plugins with audio editors and hard-disk recording systems, often used to emulate analogue and spatial effects. It is analogous to “Photoshop actions” or “Lightroom filters” in digital photography.

[We] who have an artistic approach [to music] still cling to immediacy, [like the Actionist manifesto];[^6] you are trying to create an event, and I think that is what a recording is: you are trying to [capture that event].

[^6]: Viennese Actionism (circa 1960&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&ndash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;1971) rejected object-based or otherwise commodifiable art practices by staging precisely scored “Actions” in controlled environments or before audiences. Some art historians see it as the forerunner to performance art.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2012-11-27-kali-ra-pt-1/czahller-20121013-23-09-56.jpg", "img-constrained", "David, seen from behind. His head is tilted back. He holds the microphone upside-down and above his head, pointed at his mouth. The image is black-and-white.", "David (II)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Something that really happened, not something that only occurred in the digital domain.

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> Right. You could spend a year recording and mixing an EP that is digitally perfect, but it wouldn’t have the love, the care, the attention, or the rawness of the experimental stuff from the 70s and the early 80s. You have <span class="h-card p-org">Einstürzende Neubauten</span>, you have David Bowie’s <cite>Berlin Trilogy</cite>; that was a time when people were still discovering how to do these things.

These tools shouldn’t be used as a crutch. We have to be inventive beyond them; we’ve got to define new standards.[^7]

[^7]: Goad’s lengthy discourse about process omitted for brevity.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Those are all valid concerns, but I’m hearing a lot about process and almost no mention of music. Are you a musician, or are you an artist who may include music in his art, but that’s only part of what you do?

<span class="spkr">Goad</span> Yes [, the latter]. But the entire thing is a process from end to end. You’re a parent: you conceive the child, that is, the music, you compose and go through several drafts, you’ve birthed the child, you’re rehearsing the music, and then there’re still some tweaks needed before you finally release it into the wild.
