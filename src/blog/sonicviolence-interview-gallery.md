This past <time datetime="2013-09-13">Sunday</time> the Bureau invited noise-rockers <span class="h-card p-org">SonicViolence</span>, along with video director <span class="h-card p-name">Mike Walsh</span>,[^1] to drop by for an interview in anticipation of their upcoming EP release.[^2]

[^1]: Walsh recorded video of the interview but did not participate. He appears not to have posted the recording online, but he did [interview SonicViolence](https://youtu.be/aCrV9_FEGuw?si=neVDpEW2-6nsjInW){target=_blank rel="external noopener"} himself two months later.

[^2]: The audio for the interview was not up to our usual standards, which presented some difficulties during the transcription.

<span class="spkr">Mercury Photo Bureau</span> Welcome. Please introduce yourselves.

<span class="h-card p-name spkr">Brandon Wilson</span> Brandon Wilson, and I play guitar and sing.

<span class="spkr h-card p-name">Leon Manson</span> I’m Leon Manson, and I play guitar and use my voice.

<span class="spkr h-card p-name">Terrence Mack</span> I’m Terrence Mack; I play keyboard and guitar.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> And not with us are <span class="h-card p-name">Edward Schotter</span> and <span class="h-card p-name">Donald James</span>. Donald is our drummer, and Ed is our bassist and sometimes synthesizer player.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> I read in an interview that you started the band as a duo, and that was with Ed, I believe?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> That was with Donald. Donald and I started the band. I was living in a house in Oklahoma City with 5 other guys. We actually went to high school [in Tulsa] together; but, I met him in Bricktown, in Oklahoma City [at <abbr title="Academy of Contemporary Music at the University of Central Oklahoma">ACM</abbr>]. We decided we were into a lot of the same stuff.

It was actually <span class="h-card p-name">Alan Orebaugh</span> down here in Norman&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;he plays with <span class="h-card p-org">Saucy Gentlemen’s Club</span>, and he also does the <span class="h-card p-org">Grateful Dead</span> cover band&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;[he brought us together]. The first time Donald and I jammed together was for a class [that Alan was teaching.] We were supposed to demonstrate all that we had learned on guitar for our final. Everybody else came in and played guitar solos; but for me, it was like, <q>I’m still not very good at guitar.</q> The biggest thing I know how to do is I’m good at playing with other people.

So Donald and I wrote a song together, and he came into the class. And they have drumkits in every room at ACM, so we played [the] song. And right after we finished, Alan came up and said, <q>Man, you need to start a band.</q>

I was playing in a different band at the time, so Donald and I got together and started jamming. We actually wrote a bunch of songs that I really liked; it was not [so] much the way SonicViolence sounds now; when I met Brandon a year later we basically scrapped everything so the people coming into [the band] could become more involved.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> So, you started this band before <span class="h-card p-org">[Psychic Milk](/blog/psychic-milk-interview-gallery/)</span>. Is that band still active?

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Yeah; we’re still together. But we’ve all been doing individual projects and collaborations with other people. It’s just on the back burner for now. We actually have an EP recorded and ready to go. With the momentum that <span class="h-card p-name">Justin [Hogan]</span> has gained with [Bowlsey](/blog/bowlsey-interview-gallery/), as well as the SonicViolence [EP about to drop], it probably wouldn’t be the best time to put it out.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> <span class="h-card p-name">Charlie Marsel</span> from Psychic Milk, probably the biggest thing, other than working at the comic store, is Charlie is actually writing a comic book right now. It’s gonna be called <cite>Infinity Factor</cite>, and all of the characters in it are basically all of us; it’s like all of our friends are characters in it. We’re gonna do a Psychic Milk concept album to go along with [the comic]. It’s being illustrated by Zac Cox; he’s a really awesome dude: [he does cool artwork](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ONL3EYrJ0g0){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}.

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-09-26-sonicviolence/czahller-20130614-20-24-10.jpg", "img-constrained", "Leon Manson playing guitar on the STASH patio during Friday Art Walk.", "Leon (I)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> The band name: SonicViolence. There’s an Essex, England&NoBreak;&ndash;&NoBreak;based band from the 1980s by that name.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> As soon as I thought of that name&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;which to me, is all new and all awesome and stuff; I didn’t do any research&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;I immediately went out and got the Twitter name [@sonicviolence], and I got the Bandcamp name. About a year ago, some teenage girl from Essex started going off on me [on Twitter]: <q>Your name’s not original! You’re not the original band! The *real* SonicViolence is from Essex, and they were around in the ’80s!</q> And she kept tagging this dude, <q>Can you believe this guy&#8253;</q> blah blah blah! And I went and looked at the guy she was tagging, and it turned out it was the original lead singer;[^3] I guess they’re an industrial punk band&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;I listened to a little of them on YouTube&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;he tweeted back to the girl and to me, <q>I don’t care about the name; good luck to them.</q>

[^3]: Our meticulous research team says he goes by the name “Auntie.”

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> How did the other members join?

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> T-Mack, how did you join the band?

<span class="spkr">Mack</span> I was in an experimental folk band called <span class="h-card p-org">Painted Wolf</span>, and that was kind of ending. And I was over at Brandon’s apartment in Deep Deuce[^4]. It was probably the third time I’d been asked to join SonicViolence. I was like, <q>Well, no. Yeah.</q>

[^4]: Deep Deuce is a historically African-American neighborhood in Oklahoma City, Oklahoma. Much of the district was bulldozed in the 1960s to make way for Interstate 235. It is currently undergoing a development boom, with its mix of retail and housing, arts and entertainment, and close proximity to Bricktown and Automobile Alley making it attractive for persons seeking a hip, urban vibe. At the same time, the boom is displacing the people who have historically inhabited the neighborhood.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> They wore you down.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Pretty much.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> I [*inaudible*] him hard. I wouldn’t let him not think about it for a month or two. It was always at the forefront of conversation.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> T-Mack was definitely the missing piece we really needed to make the sound full.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-09-26-sonicviolence/czahller-20130731-21-54-45.jpg", "img-constrained", "Terrence Mack smiles while he plays the synthesizer on the stage at The Conservatory", "T-Mack Smiles" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> Ed got into the band because he was my roommate at the time. He was just our default, go-to guy. He was the only logical addition to the band for the rhythm section. It just made sense at the time. <q>We need a bassist!</q> And you know, Ed’s hanging out on the couch, and we’re like, <q>Ed, [do] you have a bass?</q> and he’s like, <q>Yeah, I totally have a bass.</q> <q>Yeah, well you’re in the band, then.</q>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> How did the sound change after you expanded?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> The two-piece sound was more grunge, ’cause we were just playing in the basement&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;the whole point was it was just *super* loud. At the place where we were living, it was five [musicians], so the whole basement was filled with amps. Every time we would practice I would plug into every single amp and just try to make Donald go deaf.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> You’re still really loud, but, to my ears, it’s a lot more sophisticated than just *loud*. There’s a lot going on.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> That had a lot to do with Brandon. My thing is kind of&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;I’m not the most sophisticated sounding guitar player; a lot of my stuff is just really intense noise. But, like, when Brandon joined, he’s got lot of experience recording, a lot of experience making sounds and stuff. That’s when the attention to detail came into play. He’s our [recording] producer, too.[^5]

[^5]: Brandon also produces Psychic Milk’s recordings.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Terrence, could you tell us your musical background?

<span class="spkr">Mack</span> It started with orchestra. I went to Classen [School of Advanced Studies]; at the time I just fell in love with the sound of the organ. So I started playing organ in my dad’s church. I started organ and pretty much all keyboards when I was twelve or thirteen?

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Do you have a favorite type of organ?

<span class="spkr">Mack</span> Um, I mean, the Hammond B3, C3.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Those are the classic ones, yeah. Any of the ones that have the Leslie speaker. Can you give me a run down on Ed’s and Donald’s musical backgrounds?

<span class="spkr">Mack</span> My first band was a [contemporary] jazz band with Donald. We played one show at the Red Room here in Norman. That lasted for a while, but the bassist had to move to Chicago for school.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Ed played guitar since I met him my freshman year of high school&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;his sophomore year. He was really big into sports; [he] broke his collarbone in high school and was never really as interested in sports again.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-09-26-sonicviolence/czahller-20130731-21-53-30.jpg", "img-constrained", "Brandon Wilson plays guitar on on the stage at The Conservatory", "Brandon" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> I don’t remember who it was, but I interviewed somebody else who had a sports injury and couldn’t play any more, and that’s when he took up whatever instrument it was.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> Ed is definitely our resident jock. Every show we play, he’s wearing a different soccer jersey.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Can you tell me about writing songs for the band?

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> It’s definitely a collaboration. The first song we wrote together&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;we wrote it in stages, as a collective, and then in subsets of the collective&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;is <cite class="short-work">Overmedicated and Underutilized.</cite> That’s really our first group effort. But we also have songs that have really strong contributions [from the various band members].

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> After we put this EP out, we’re gonna go straight to work on a [full-length] record. It’s gonna be all new songs. There’s been a lot of stuff, while we’ve been recording, putting this new stuff out&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;I think it’s easy as a band to get ahead of yourself, and not have all of your stuff recorded, and some stuff falls by the wayside. But the way this band has always worked is, we don’t want to put anything out before it’s ready. And we’re real big into getting everything just perfect. I guess, not as much live, because we’ve tried out things&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;tested the waters&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;playing live, but we’re maniacal about getting things to sound perfect. That’s why it’s taken so long to get this EP out. The single off of this EP, Brandon pretty much wrote it in its entirety. We’ve been playing it for almost a year.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> I wanted to ask you about the single. First of all, it’s got a great title: <cite class="short-work">One Day We Are All Going to Explode (and it will be beautiful).</cite> This was your first single?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> Pretty much. We released it as a demo. We had done that first EP with [Trent Bell](https://belllabsrecording.com/){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}; it was good, but it wasn’t necessarily the way we wanted it to sound. We had two days to record it. We tracked it the first day, mixed it the next day, and it came out sounding kind of plain. It was very clean, which is not necessarily the sound we go for.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Here’s my take on the single. I hear a unison vocal, an octave apart throughout. I don’t think there’s any harmony singing. It’s very melodic; I’m thinking “power pop guitar,” but then as it progresses, it devolves into a “noisy” chorus, while still maintaining the melody in the vocal line. Then the whole thing just goes to a crashing, feedback-laden ending. Anything you want to say about the song itself?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> We went up to Brandon’s house, in Tulsa. He’s got a really nice “live” room to record in there. We were messing around, recording different stuff, and it was just this idea that I came up with then. We didn’t go there to track the song. I just started playing the verse guitar line and then it just went from there, with Brandon coming in and saying, <q>Oh, well, maybe *this* will sound cool if this was *this* way,</q> and then we figured out all the stuff and tracked it that night. The whole thing took maybe, five hours, to write and record.

I was kind of bummed out when we went up there&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;I think that’s a common theme in my lyrics, that they’re all depressing&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;but it was weird, because I was writing these really depressing lyrics, but the whole time we were recording that song, I have never been happier. It was a blast.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-09-26-sonicviolence/czahller-20130614-20-24-53.jpg", "img-constrained", "Donald James plays drums on the STASH patio during Friday Art Walk", "Donald" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

The thing with the way I write lyrics is, I do it from a stream-of-consciousness standpoint; I mean, I’ll be listening to [the music], and I’ll just start saying stuff, and if it fits, it fits; and if it doesn’t, it doesn’t. I’ll look back at it, and it’s like I’m giving myself therapy.

That song is close to my heart, because it was written with everybody there. A lot of times I’ll write songs where it’ll just be me, awake at <time datetime="03:00-05:00">3:00 in the morning</time>, with just my guitar. But I think the best songs we’ve written are when we’re all together and just feeling really inspired and spontaneous. That’s when the people you work with will come through and have something awesome, every time.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> You moved from a sort of “grunge” sound to a kind of “noise-rock” sound. It’s really hard to pin a genre on non-commercial bands these days, but you’ve cited some influences. [Dinosaur Jr.](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/artist/dinosaur-jr./id509577?uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"} and [Radiohead](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/artist/radiohead/id657515?uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}, for instance.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> It’s never like we want to sound like this one band. It’s more like, that guitar tone in this particular song by that band is really sick, and that inspires me to go do my own version of it.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> You’ve warned people to bring earplugs to your shows, and I can attest to that. Mostly because of you guys and the [Kamals](/blog/kamals-interview-gallery/), I’ve started taking earplugs to the shows I photograph. [*laughter*] Your Facebook page says your sound is like <cite>How High</cite> meets <cite>2001: A Space Odyssey</cite>. I didn’t really get that; I mean, I don’t really hear much in the way of hip-hop?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> Donald’s drumming is very hip-hop. That’s something we really go for, is that backing beat. Especially on that first song, which is a tune that Brandon wrote, <cite class="short-work">Afterbirth</cite>; like, the whole thing sounds like a super [*inaudible*].

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> So, does he bring in funk and pre-hip-hop elements, too?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> I think so, yeah; it definitely shows in his fills. I think the whole <cite>How High</cite> meets <cite>2001: A Space Odyssey</cite> thing comes from, <cite>How High</cite> is this super-grungy hip-hop movie, that is kinda fucked up, and kinda raunchy in parts, and <cite>2001: A Space Odyssey</cite> is this grand vision of&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> <span class="foreign" lang="fr">Avant garde</span> interpretation.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> So you’re taking something very earthy and putting this intellectual spin on it?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> At least, speaking for me, I want to make crazy noise-rock with really intense production that sounds amazing, and people can listen to and go, <q>Oh, wow; it’s crazy, how this part goes *there*,</q> and that’s definitely Brandon’s thing.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-09-26-sonicviolence/czahller-20130731-21-47-31.jpg", "img-constrained", "Ed Schotter plays bass on the stage at The Conservatory", "Ed (I)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> I don’t think we aspire to be intellectual or <span class="foreign" lang="fr">avant garde</span>; just ambiguous. Open to some interpretation. A lot of my lyrics weave metaphor with the literal.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> Brandon and I pretty much [divide the lyrics writing evenly]. I’ve always been the guy who, like, I’ll listen to a song, and I’ll make up lyrics to it, whether they’re right or not. And I think that’s cool; like, I want people to not know what the lyrics [to our songs] are, make them up for themselves, and then have their own interpretations.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> There’s not a lot of open space in your songs. Do you see yourselves opening up the sound, sonically, any time in the future?

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Definitely. Especially on the album as opposed to the upcoming EP, because we’re still in this dense style of making music that has as much in it as possible that doesn’t sound completely cluttered.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Like Danny Elfman.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Absolutely. The “more” is not necessarily “less” approach.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> The new stuff, especially <cite class="short-work">Overmedicated and Underutilized,</cite> sounds a lot more sparse. As far as the really heavy guitar tones go, this is the least heavy thing we’ve recorded. The guitars are mixed a lot more back; drums and bass are more in front. Some of it has a pseudo-industrial vibe to it.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> There’s a lot more synths and keys, as well. This is our first recording with Terrence.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> I understand that you’ll have some demo copies of the EP at the <span class="h-card p-org">[Wavves](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/artist/wavves/id302202865?uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}</span> show at the Conservatory.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> We’ll have at least one song on CD ready to go.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> You’ll be opening for Wavves with [Skating Polly](/blog/skating-polly-interview-pt-1/). There seems to be a tremendous amount of excitement in the music fan community for the show.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> Wavves has been one of my favorite bands since I was a high school freshman. He did a bunch of super lo-fi tape releases before moving to a full band and making more produced sounding albums. It’s pretty much gonna be the biggest show we’ve ever played.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Production-wise, my favorite two records of <time datetime="2013">2013</time> are <cite>[Afraid of Heights](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/album/afraid-of-heights/id596122320?uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}</cite> by Wavves and <cite>[Trouble Will Find Me](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/album/trouble-will-find-me/id626872826?uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}</cite> by <span class="h-card p-org">The National</span>.

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-09-26-sonicviolence/czahller-20130614-20-22-07.jpg", "img-constrained", "Terrence (keyboard), Brandon (guitar), Donald (drums), and Ed (bass) play on the STASH patio during Friday Art Walk. The stage consists of stacked wooden pallets.", "SonicViolence (III)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Do you see music as a career, or is it going to turn out to be that thing you got to do when you were in your twenties?

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> It’s all I know how to do.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Me too.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> My friend’s dad, Basile Kolliopoulos from the <span class="h-card p-org">[Fortune Tellers](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/artist/fortune-tellers/id83511640?uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}</span>&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;he worked hard all his life, bunch of different record deals, bunch of different opportunities, but he always turned them down because they wouldn’t let him do what *he* wanted to do. But he literally played music until he died late last year. And he did it while supporting and raising a family.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> We’ll wrap up by redeeming a song, where we talk about a song that doesn’t get much respect, but that you love, and you tell me what’s great about it. Leon and Brandon already got to do this in the Psychic Milk interview, but are welcome to play again.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> My reappraisal of the question led me to <cite class="short-work">[Believe](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/album/believe/id73273758?i=73273491&uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"},</cite> by <span class="h-card p-name">Cher</span>.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> That’s the one with Auto-Tune?&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;or at least with Auto-Tune cranked all the way up?

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> It was the first song with *any* kind of Auto-Tune.[^6] The Antares Auto-Tune software was used on a singer who already had "pipes" and could sing [on pitch]&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;purely as an effect. That’s so fascinating to me, because it’s the <span class="foreign" lang="la">status quo</span> now. If you look at what was playing on Top 40 radio at that time&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;it heralded the return of electro-house. That was the era of <span class="h-card p-org">Third Eye Blind</span> and <span class="h-card p-org">Marcy’s Playground</span> and a bunch of pseudo-slacker rock bands that were adding more "hook" elements to their songs. And here was this thing that was *totally* synthetic; sample-centric, artificial&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

[^6]: Aphex Twin recorded <cite class="short-work">Funny Little Man</cite> in <time datetime="1997-10">October 1997</time> using Auto-Tune, predating Cher’s use of the technology by a year.

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> Heavy on the rhythm section&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Exactly. It really was ahead of its time. It’s fascinating; [Cher] was basically a one-hit wonder, barring her early career [as half of the musical duo <span class="h-card p-org">Sonny &amp; Cher</span>]&NoBreak;&hairsp;&NoBreak;&mdash;

<span class="spkr">MPB</span> She was probably better known as a actress at the time.

<span class="spkr">Wilson</span> Yeah; I find that phenomenal, and something that can’t *not* go down in history.

<span class="spkr">Manson</span> Mine is <cite class="short-work">[Brain Stew](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/album/brain-stew/1160079960?i=1160080224&mt=1&app=music&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}</cite> by <span class="h-card p-org">Green Day</span>. I think that’s one of the most hated and underrated songs. It’s all power chords; it was one of the first CDs I ever owned, and it was the first song I ever learned how to play on guitar; I’m always going to love that song. Like, it’s got *such* a good chord progression!

<mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2013-09-26-sonicviolence/czahller-20130614-20-34-29.jpg", "img-constrained", "Closeup of Terrence’s hands on the keyboard.", "Terrence (Moog Detail)" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

<span class="spkr">Mack</span> <cite class="short-work">[#41](https://geo.itunes.apple.com/us/album/41/id388136111?i=388136676&uo=4&at=10ldhk){target=_blank rel="external noopener"}</cite> by Dave Matthews. I know the <span class="h-card p-org"><span class="h-card p-name">Dave Matthews</span> Band</span> gets a lot of crap, but sometimes all I need to hear is <cite class="short-work">#41.</cite> It’s just so groovy; Carter Beauford is a really great drummer, and I actually enjoy the vocals.
