# {{ title }}

<p>An Architectural Gem on the Oklahoma Prairie</p></stack-l></hgroup>
<!-- markdownlint-enable MD025 --><mpb-dialog-img>

{% image "./src/assets/images/blog/2019-04-24-prairie-house/czahller-20170521-16-26-25.jpg", "img-constrained", "Southwest View — Prairie House, Norman, Oklahoma", "Southwest View — Prairie House, Norman, Oklahoma" %}</mpb-dialog-img>

In <time datetime="1957">1957</time> a young architect named <span class="h-card p-name">Herb Greene</span> returned to his alma mater, the <span class="h-card p-org">University of Oklahoma</span>, to teach. Working alongside his former professor (modernist <span class="h-card p-name">Bruce Goff</span>) and others, he helped develop the <span class="h-card p-org">American School</span> of architecture. Described as <q>A new school, probably the only indigenous one in the United States</q> by co-founder <span class="h-card p-name">Donald MacDonald</span>, the curriculum eschewed the pedagogy of the French Beaux Arts and Bauhaus schools, instead approaching architecture and its pedagogy in an <q>original and authentically American fashion</q> by emphasizing <q>individual creativity, organic forms, and experimentation.</q>&NoBreak;[^1]

[^1]: <cite class="h-card p-name full-citation">Pilat, Stephanie,</cite> <cite class="short-work"><a href="https://stephaniepilat.oucreate.com/uncategorized/the-american-school-of-architecture-building-on-the-plains/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">The American School of Architecture: Building on the Plains</a>,</cite> <cite>stephaniepilat<wbr>.oucreate<wbr>.com</cite> (<time datetime="2017-02-12">12 February 2017</time>).

## The Prairie Chicken

While teaching at the university, Greene purchased a small plot of land east of Norman and designed and built a home for his young family. The home, now known as <strong>The Prairie House</strong>,&NoBreak;[^2] was completed in <time datetime="1961">1961</time>.

[^2]: When <cite>Look</cite> and <cite>Life</cite> magazines featured the home in articles in <time datetime="1962">1962</time> they dubbed it the <q>Prairie Chicken House.</q> Perhaps the most famous photographs of the house were taken by <span class="h-card p-name"><a href="https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/kt1779r539/admin/#aspace_8f13af5a7d5f72669089af58d336afc1" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">Julius Shulman</a></span>. Best known for his <cite>Case Study</cite> photographs, Shulman <a href="https://primo.getty.edu/primo-explore/search?query=any,contains,shulman%20oklahoma&tab=all_gri&search_scope=COMBINED&vid=GRI&sortby=rank&lang=en_US" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">frequently traveled to Oklahoma to document its midcentury architecture</a>.

Situated on two acres with no other homes in view, the house featured <q>natural materials, passive design, natural lighting and ventilation, energy efficiency, and careful site placement.</q>&NoBreak;[^3] The rough cedar shingles covering the the home inside and out give it the look of a shaggy, living thing. Greene says he wanted the house to look as though it were struggling to climb from the flat landscape, <q>Like it’s coming into the ground and in some views trying to free itself from the ground.</q>&NoBreak;[^4]

[^3]: <cite class="h-card p-name full-citation">Laylin, Tafline,</cite> <cite class="short-work"><a href="https://inhabitat.com/1960s-prairie-house-by-herb-greene-is-an-early-example-of-green-design/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">Herb Greene’s Crazy 1960s Prairie Chicken House Was a Green Design Before Its Time</a>,</cite> <cite>inhabit<wbr>.com</cite> (<time datetime="2011-08-23">23 August 2011</time>).

[^4]: <cite class="h-card p-name full-citation">Culver, Galen,</cite> <cite class="short-work"><a href="https://inhabitat.com/1960s-prairie-house-by-herb-greene-is-an-early-example-of-green-design/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">The Prairie House Architect Comes Home to See His Famous Structure for the First Time in More than 50 Years</a>,</cite> <cite>KFOR<wbr>.com</cite> (<time datetime="2017-04-17">17 April 2017</time>).

Greene and his family lived in the house for only a year-and-a-half. The <span class="h-card p-org">University of Kentucky</span> offered him a professorship, and for the next half century the house was home to local businesswoman <span class="h-card p-name">Janie Wilson</span>,&NoBreak;[^5] who lived in it until her death in <time datetime="2016-01">2016</time>. Business owner <span class="h-card p-name">Brent Swift</span> acquired the property soon after.

[^5]: It was Wilson who planted the trees that now obscure the house from passersby.

## New Life

The house has since undergone extensive repairs and renovations. When the bureau visited the property in <time datetime="2017-05-21">2017</time>, <span class="h-card p-org">Brent Swift Architecture</span> and <span class="h-card p-org">Butzer Architects and Urbanism</span> were involved in the restoration, with Greene informally consulting.&NoBreak;[^6] <time datetime="2018">Since then</time> <span class="h-card p-name">Austin Hacker</span> and <span class="h-card p-name">Bryan Bloom</span> of design firm <span class="h-card p-org">OXBloom</span> have acquired the property from Swift and are continuing renovations.&NoBreak;[^7] In partnership with the <span class="h-card p-org">Tower Theatre</span> they have <a href="https://www.facebook.com/events/278207109721869/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">instituted a concert series hosted in the historic building</a>.

[^6]: <cite class="h-card p-name full-citation">Rostochil, Lynne</cite>, <cite class="short-work"><a href="https://okcmod.com/2017/08/herb-greene-visits-norman-and-his-prairie-house/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">Herb Greene Visits Norman and His Prairie House</a>,</cite> <cite>OKC MOD</cite> (<time datetime="2017-08-03">3 August 2017</time>).

[^7]: <cite class="h-card p-name full-citation">Azzarello, Nina</cite>, <cite class="short-work"><a href="https://www.designboom.com/architecture/prairie-house-herb-greene-austin-hacker-bryan-bloom-renovation-09-22-2018/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">Step inside the 1960s ‘Prairie House’ in Oklahoma Collaged in Swirling Cedar Shingles</a>,</cite> <cite>designboom</cite> (<time datetime="2018-09-23">23 September 2018</time>).

## History in Peril

Oklahoma is a treasure-trove of mid-century modern architecture, but in recent years many of the state’s iconic structures have met the wrecking ball. <span class="h-card p-org">Stage Center</span>, <strong>Union Bus Station</strong>, <strong><span class="h-card p-name p-family-name">Bavinger</span> House</strong>, and <span class="h-card p-org">Founders Bank</span> are all gone Also endangered but not yet gone are the <span class="h-card p-org">Citizens Bank Gold Dome</span> and the <span class="h-card p-org">Central National Motor Bank</span>, while Oklahoma City’s <span class="h-card p-org">First Christian Church</span> (<abbr>a.k.a.</abbr> "The Egg") just narrowly avoided demolition when an area church <a href="https://newsok.com/article/5629381/crossing-church-announces-talks-with-first-christian-church-to-open-satellite-campus" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">agreed to buy the property</a> <time datetime="2019-04-21">earlier this week</time>.&NoBreak;[^8] The <a href="https://okcmod.com/2019/02/mid-century-modern-on-preservation-oklahomas-most-endangered-places-list/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">Okie Mod Squad</a> have compiled a list of endangered structures, past and present, as well as <a href="https://okcmod.com/resources/preservation/" target="_blank" rel="external noopener">links to preservation resources</a>.

[^8]: Since the publication of this article the church has been demolished.

## Gallery

When my friend <span class="h-card p-name">Joe Crumley</span> and I photographed the Prairie House I brought along a Hasselblad <abbr>500C</abbr> that I intended to use. I changed my mind and instead borrowed Joe’s Mamiya 645. While not as well-made as the Swedish Hasselblad, the Mamiya 645 is much more ergonomic and produces beautiful images. Unlike the 1:1 square format of the Hasselblad, the Mamiya takes 2:3 (6<abbr>cm</abbr> &times; 4.5<abbr>cm</abbr>) images.
